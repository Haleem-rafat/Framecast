# The assessment landing page

A structure for a landing page whose job is to turn a stranger into a
qualified, contactable lead — and the assessment funnel behind it.

The claim it rests on: **everything is downstream of lead generation.** A
business that generates leads can solve its other problems in whatever order it
likes; a business that cannot is stuck regardless of how good the product is.
So the landing page is not a brochure. It has exactly one job, and every block
on it either moves someone toward the next step or is in the way.

**The benchmark to hold yourself to: 20–40% of people who hit the page start
the assessment.** If a page is not built to be measured against a number like
that, it is a brochure with a button on it.

---

## Part 1 — The landing page: four blocks, in this order

Do not reorder them. Each one answers the question the previous one creates.

### Block 1 — The hook

The first thing on the screen, above everything. Exactly one of two forms:

**The frustration hook.** Name the frustration, and name it *with* the effort
the reader is already making. The template is:

> *Feeling frustrated that **[the result you are not getting]** even though
> **[the effort you are already putting in]**?*

Examples from the source: *"frustrated that you're not getting the fitness
results you want even though you work out every week"*; *"frustrated that
you're not getting investment returns even though you invest consistently every
month"*; *"frustrated that you have not been able to scale up your marketing
even though you're willing to invest in ads."*

The "even though" clause is the whole mechanism. Without it the sentence blames
the reader; with it, it tells them the problem is not their effort. That is why
it lands.

**The results hook**, written as a readiness question:

> *Are you ready to **[the result]**?*

*"Are you ready to run your first marathon?" "Are you ready to have the
relationship of your dreams?" "Are you ready to protect your business from a
cyber security threat?"*

Why it works: a readiness question does not invite yes or no, it invites *I'm
not sure — I want to find out.* That uncertainty is what the assessment
resolves, so the question and the CTA are the same motion.

**The subheading directs them into the assessment**, and says how long it is:

> *Answer 15 questions to find out why you're experiencing this and what to do
> about it.*

### Block 2 — The value proposition

Three things you will **measure and improve**. Not features. Not adjectives.
Three nameable areas the reader would be pleased to improve on the way to the
result.

> *Answer these 15 questions so we can measure and improve your **[area 1]**,
> your **[area 2]** and your **[area 3]**.*

A sleep assessment measures sleep environment, sleep routine, sleep nutrition.
Pick three that a reader recognises as the real components of their problem —
the list itself demonstrates that you understand it.

### Block 3 — Credibility

A strong hook plus a strong value proposition sounds too good to be true, and
the reader's next thought is *who are you?* Answer it before they leave to find
out. Three things:

1. **Who created this** — you, and your bio.
2. **What you have done before** — track record, in specifics.
3. **What research it is built on** — a statistic, a study, a number.
   *"85% of people struggle with X." "A recent study found 15% achieve Y."*

Credibility is not a logo wall. It is the answer to "why should the number this
thing gives me mean anything?"

### Block 4 — The call to action

Four elements, together. The combination is what converts, not any one of them:

1. **The next step, named as itself** — *Start the quiz.* Not "learn more",
   not "get started". The literal next action.
2. **How long it takes** — *It only takes 3 minutes.*
3. **That it is free** — *Completely free.*
4. **What they get immediately** — *You get immediate recommendations on how
   to improve.*

Time, price and payoff, all before the click. Each one removes a reason to
hesitate, and the reader is not asked to trust you for any of them.

## Part 2 — The assessment: 15 questions in three parts

### Contact (before the questions)

- **Name** — required
- **Email** — required
- **Location** — captured automatically from IP, never asked
- **Phone** — offered, **never mandatory**

Two required fields. Every extra required field is a lead you did not get.

### The 10 best-practice questions

Ten things a person *should* be doing if they were doing everything right. Each
answer is a yes or a no, and the count is the score.

Write them by asking: what would someone who has already solved this be doing?
*Do they get to bed at the same time? Do they use screens before bed? Do they
track calories? Do they train three or four times a week?*

These do double duty — they produce the score, and they teach the reader what
good looks like while they answer. Someone who answers "no" eight times has
just diagnosed themselves.

### The 5 qualifying questions

These are what make the leads sellable. Ask all five, in this order:

| # | Question | What it tells you |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Which best describes your **current situation**? (4–5 options) | Where they are — segment |
| 2 | Which **desired outcome** matters most in the next 90 days? | What is driving the decision |
| 3 | What **obstacle** is stopping you / what have you tried that did not work? | The objection, pre-stated |
| 4 | Which **solution** would suit you best? (book / course / coaching / software / done-for-you) | **Budget, implied** |
| 5 | **Is there anything else we should know?** (open box) | Everything you did not think to ask |

Question 4 is the commercial one: someone who wants a book is thinking $10–20;
someone who wants it all done for them is thinking $10,000–20,000. You learn
budget without asking about money.

Question 5 is the one people underestimate. After answering fourteen questions
a person is invested, and they volunteer things like *"I've already read your
book and want to get started"* or *"I have a budget but must spend it by the
end of the month."*

## Part 3 — Dynamic results

The results page **changes based on the answers**. That is the point of it, and
it is the difference between an assessment and a form.

1. **The big reveal** — the overall score, shown as a thing not a number:
   a dial, a thermometer, hot/warm/cold. Then a sentence that matches it:
   *"Congratulations, you scored well."* / *"You have strong foundations and
   plenty of room to improve."* / *"There is a lot to fix here."*
2. **Three insights** — three things they should know, chosen by how they
   answered.
3. **Next steps, branched by qualification** — and this is where the money is:
   - **Highly qualified** → offer a one-to-one meeting
   - **Middle** → offer a group event or presentation
   - **Wrong fit** → give them content: a video, a podcast, a book
4. **Contact details and links** at the bottom, so a reader who wants more has
   somewhere to go that is not "back".

## How to apply this to a product landing page

Most product pages are not assessments, and the funnel above does not
transplant wholesale. The **four blocks always apply**; the quiz is a separate
product decision. When adapting:

- The hook still names a frustration with an "even though" clause, or asks a
  readiness question.
- The value proposition still names **three** measurable things. For a product,
  these are the three things the product improves — stated as outcomes, not
  features.
- Credibility still answers "why should I believe this" with specifics, not
  adjectives.
- The CTA still carries **all four** elements: the literal next step, the time
  it takes, the price, and what happens immediately.

The most common failure is a page that has all four blocks in spirit but states
none of them plainly — a hook that describes the product instead of the
reader's frustration, a value proposition that lists features, credibility made
of adjectives, and a CTA that says "Get started" with no time, no price and no
promise.

## What this structure is not

- **Not a brochure.** Every block moves toward one action. If a section does
  not, it is decoration.
- **Not a feature list.** Features belong below the fold, after the four blocks
  have done their job.
- **Not a place for hedging.** "It only takes 3 minutes and it's completely
  free" is a commitment. If you cannot make it, fix the thing rather than
  softening the sentence.
